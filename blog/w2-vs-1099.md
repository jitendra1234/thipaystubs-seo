When you pay someone for work, one of the first questions is whether they are an **employee** or an **independent contractor**. The answer decides which tax forms you file, what you withhold from their pay, and which taxes your business owes.

## The short version

| | W-2 employee | 1099 contractor |
|---|---|---|
| Who controls the work | The employer | The worker |
| Taxes withheld from pay | Federal income tax, Social Security, Medicare, state tax | None |
| Employer pays payroll taxes | Yes (employer share of FICA, plus unemployment taxes) | No |
| Year-end form | Form W-2 | Form 1099-NEC |
| Form the worker gives you | Form W-4 | Form W-9 |

## W-2 employees

An employee works under your direction: you decide when, where and how the work is done, provide the tools, and usually pay a regular wage or salary. For employees you:

- collect a **Form W-4** so you know how much federal income tax to withhold,
- withhold federal income tax, Social Security, Medicare and any state or local taxes from every paycheck,
- pay the employer's share of Social Security and Medicare, plus federal and state unemployment taxes,
- report wages and withholding each quarter on **Form 941**, and
- give each employee a **Form W-2** after the year ends, and send the W-2s to the Social Security Administration (with a **Form W-3** transmittal when filing on paper).

Employees also receive a [pay stub](/create-pay-stub) with each paycheck showing how their pay was calculated.

## 1099 contractors

An independent contractor runs their own business: they control how the work is done, often use their own tools, may work for several clients, and are paid by the job or invoice. For contractors you:

- collect a **Form W-9** with their name and taxpayer identification number,
- do **not** withhold income tax or FICA (they pay their own self-employment tax), and
- report what you paid them on **Form 1099-NEC** after the year ends, if the payments are above the IRS reporting threshold for that year.

## How to classify a worker

The IRS looks at three areas:

1. **Behavioral control:** does the business control what the worker does and how they do it?
2. **Financial control:** who pays expenses, provides tools, and carries the chance of profit or loss?
3. **Relationship:** is there a written contract, are benefits provided, and is the work a key, ongoing part of the business?

Many states apply stricter tests, so check your state's rules too. Misclassifying an employee as a contractor can lead to back taxes, penalties and interest. If you are unsure, the IRS Form SS-8 process or a tax professional can help.

If you run payroll in Canada, every paycheque has the same four main deductions: **Canada Pension Plan (CPP)** contributions, the newer **CPP2** contributions, **Employment Insurance (EI)** premiums, and **income tax**, both federal and provincial. This guide explains each one and how they appear on a pay stub, also called a pay statement.

## Gross pay first

Start with gross pay for the period: hours × hourly rate for hourly staff, or annual salary divided by the number of pay periods for salaried staff (52 for weekly, 26 for biweekly, 24 for semi-monthly, 12 for monthly). Add overtime, vacation pay, bonuses and taxable benefits.

## Canada Pension Plan (CPP)

CPP is a contributory pension. Employees and employers each contribute the same percentage of the employee's pensionable earnings.

- Contributions are charged on earnings **above a basic exemption of $3,500 a year** and **up to the Year's Maximum Pensionable Earnings (YMPE)**.
- The exemption is spread across the year, so each pay period gets its share.
- Once an employee reaches the yearly maximum contribution, CPP stops for the rest of the year.

The CRA sets the rate and YMPE every year.

## CPP2 (second additional CPP contributions)

Since 2024, employees who earn more than the YMPE also pay **CPP2** on earnings between the YMPE and a higher ceiling called the **Year's Additional Maximum Pensionable Earnings (YAMPE)**. The employer matches it. Most lower and middle earners never reach CPP2, so it only appears on stubs once earnings pass the YMPE.

## Employment Insurance (EI)

EI premiums fund benefits such as job-loss, maternity and parental leave.

- The employee pays a percentage of insurable earnings, up to the **Maximum Insurable Earnings** for the year.
- The employer pays **1.4 times** the employee's premium.
- Like CPP, EI stops once the yearly maximum is reached.

## Income tax: federal and provincial

Income tax is withheld based on the employee's **TD1** forms, one federal and one provincial, which set their personal tax credits. Federal tax and provincial tax are calculated separately using each government's brackets. Ontario also has the **Ontario Health Premium**, which is withheld with provincial tax.

## Quebec is different

Quebec has its own pension plan (QPP) instead of CPP, its own parental insurance plan (QPIP), a lower EI rate, and provincial tax that is reported separately to Revenu Québec. Quebec payroll has its own rules and forms.

## What goes on a Canadian pay stub

A complete Canadian pay statement usually shows:

- employer name and address, and the employee's name;
- pay period and pay date;
- hours and rates, or salary, and gross pay;
- each deduction on its own line: CPP, CPP2 if any, EI, federal tax and provincial tax;
- other deductions such as benefits or RRSP contributions;
- net pay;
- year-to-date totals, which show when CPP and EI maximums have been reached.

Employment standards in most provinces require employers to give a pay statement every payday.
